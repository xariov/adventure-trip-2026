Flights

The way in is booked: Jetstar Brisbane-Sydney on 1 September, then one Scoot ticket Sydney-Singapore-Vienna on the 2nd and 3rd (departs Sydney 13:00, lands Changi 19:30, out again around 02:45, lands Vienna about 09:00; checked bags ride through, and the layover is long enough for the Tampines climbing session on the itinerary). What remains is the flight home from Athens on Tuesday 6 or Wednesday 7 October, and the two researched builds land exactly on those days.

The simple version: Emirates one-way Athens-Brisbane via Dubai, about AUD 1,500-1,670 each, cheapest on the Tuesday, 30 kg included, no assembly. The cheaper version, in keeping with the Scoot theme: Scoot's 08:55 Athens-Singapore on Wednesday 7 October (it runs Monday, Wednesday, Friday and Sunday until 18 October) plus a Singapore Airlines nonstop to Brisbane the next morning, about AUD 1,160-1,520 each including a 30 kg bag bought at booking, with a six-to-eight-hour Changi buffer to collect and re-check. Singapore's website rarely sells the two as one ticket, so book them as a pair: Scoot direct (price-check 7 Oct) and Singapore Airlines for the morning hop (8 Oct). The Athens night before the early departure is already in the plan.

The hire cars

Two rentals: the Dolomites week (pick up 4 September, drop Trento on the 10th or 11th) and the Slovenia week (Ljubljana, round trip, ~19-24 September). For the Dolomites the pickup follows the Vienna branch: Bolzano's city offices if we train down (small cars ran €30-45 a day at the July check, with Noleggiare baking the one-way fee into the rate), or Bergamo airport if the Milan evening happens (a bigger, cheaper market, typically €22-35 a day, and a one-way to Trento is an ordinary domestic drop). Ljubljana priced €18-32 a day for economy cars in late September with the motorway vignette included, cheapest at the railway-station branches. Live quotes: Bergamo to Trento, Bolzano to Trento, Ljubljana, plus DiscoverCars and Localrent for the local-firm rates.

Choosing the car and the booking: the smallest class that swallows two duffels and two packs is right for the passes (a Clio or Panda class does it; one size up buys a parcel shelf that hides everything), confirm unlimited kilometres since the Arabba week will run 600-900 km, and take the second-driver option (about €9.50 a day at the majors, free on some broker cars) because six days of hairpins is better shared. The deposit needs a real credit card in the driver's name with €800-1,500 of headroom; debit cards get rejected or strong-armed into desk insurance, which is the trap the whole next paragraph exists to avoid.

Add-ons, sorted by whether they matter. Collision damage waiver and theft protection are already bundled into Italian and Slovenian rates by default, so "theft insurance" as such is not a purchase decision; what the bundle leaves you carrying is the excess, typically €800-1,500 per incident, and the desk will offer to erase it for €15-30 a day, which is €90-200 per rental. The verified better answer is already in our insurance shortlist: Freely's top plan carries rental vehicle excess cover to $10,000, and insure4less carries it at $2,000 on Excel and $6,000 on Excel Plus (nothing on the lower tiers), covering the excess after an accident or theft while we drive. So the play is: decline the desk cover, let the travel policy carry the excess, and know the mechanics: the rental company charges the excess to the card first and the insurer reimburses on paperwork. Two genuine caveats. Tyres, glass and underbody often sit outside the rental company's waiver entirely, and pass driving is exactly where gravel finds windscreens; a standalone excess policy (DiscoverCars' full-coverage add-on or RentalCover, €6-9 a day) explicitly includes them and is the cheap middle path if that worries us. And theft cover is only as good as our habits: the real exposure is two climbing kits visible in a trailhead boot, so the ropes live at the Arabba apartment on hiking days, not in the car. Skip without guilt: GPS, prepaid fuel, personal accident cover (the travel policy owns that), premium roadside. Fuel policy full-to-full only, and mind the ZTL camera zones when returning in Trento; follow the rental-return signage, not the shortest line on the map.

On rooms: the daily figures assume a twin, which prices the same as a double everywhere in Europe ("double" in the tables above is the room class, not the bed count). Where separate rooms or beds cost about the same, we take them: two studios on Kalymnos run EUR 50-90 a night total, and Montenegrin guesthouses are cheap enough to just book two. In the Dolomites and Arco separate rooms add roughly 30-60% to the room line, so there it is a twin or a two-bedroom apartment, and a shared double stays in reserve for the places where it is significantly cheaper or the only sensible option. In the rifugios everyone is in dorm bunks anyway. Rate-plan rule for the booking session: Ortisei and Arco happen regardless of every open decision, so cheap non-refundable rates are safe there; the Cortina-side nights hinge on the hut answers and Kalymnos hinges on festival plans, so those two take free-cancellation rates only. Insurance: answered, via ferrata included

The two questions were what climbing cover costs, and whether via ferrata needs something beyond a policy that covers sport climbing. It does. On current published wording, no mainstream Australian policy covers unguided via ferrata at Dolomites altitude: Cover-More and Freely both name via ferrata but cap it at 2,000 m, which fails Sass Rigais at 3,025 m; World Nomads names it uncapped but requires a professional guide for both via ferrata and sport climbing; Fast Cover and SCTI require licensed operators; InsureandGo and Tick name via ferrata specifically to exclude it.

The exception is insure4less, and it was live-quoted at our ages (35 and 42) on 22 July for the exact dates and all four countries. Via ferrata is automatically included on every tier, uncapped, with no guide condition, and the Rock Climbing Plan add-on covers unguided bolted sport climbing to 3,500 m. Base policy for both of us, 37 days: $298-436 depending on tier; the climbing add-on is $20.83 per person per day for only the days we nominate, so a ten-day climbing window adds about $417. Call it $730-855 total for the pair, everything on one policy. The fine print that matters: helmets mandatory at the crag, a $500 excess on climbing medical claims, no physiotherapy cover, search-and-rescue only on the top (Excel Plus) tier, and a mixed claims reputation. It is single-trip and must be bought before leaving Australia. Also worth knowing: their age bands step at 36, not 40, so crossing 40 changes nothing (the 42-year-old pays about $41 more than the 35-year-old on the mid tier).

The belt-and-braces addition, worthwhile whichever base policy wins: Austrian Alpine Club membership through the UK Britannia section, GBP 65 plus GBP 50 for a partner rate (about AUD 220-250 for the pair, joinable by Australians, covering immediately). It covers via ferrata and unguided climbing outright, with EUR 25,000 mountain rescue per person and unlimited repatriation in the first eight weeks of a trip, which are exactly the catastrophic items retail policies condition away, plus discounts at the Alta Via rifugios. Its cap is EUR 10,000 of foreign hospital cover, and Australians cannot buy the premium top-up; in Italy the Australia-Italy reciprocal health agreement (Medicare card plus passport, public hospitals) largely plugs that, and Italy is where the via ferrata days are.

The rest of the trip's activities fall out cleanly under insure4less: trekking is automatically covered to 4,000 m without a guide, whitewater rafting to grade 4, and canyoning when run by a commercial operator, which the Soča trips are. Emergency dental, verified in the policy documents (13 Aug): insure4less pays up to $500 for dentist-certified acute pain relief on healthy natural teeth (and its $500 climbing excess could swallow that if the tooth breaks at the crag; ask them before buying); Freely carries a dedicated overseas dental benefit up to $2,000 on the top plan; the Alpine Club covers first-response pain-relief dentistry inside its medical ceiling. All three exclude routine and pre-existing dental, so any grumbling tooth sees an Australian dentist in August.
